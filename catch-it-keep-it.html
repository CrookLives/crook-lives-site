Catch It, Keep It

October 2, 2026

Inspiration doesn't make appointments. It doesn't wait until you're sitting down with a clear schedule and a fresh cup of coffee. It shows up in the middle of a drive. It shows up in a line someone says in passing, in a beat bleeding out of a car window, in a color on a wall you've walked past a hundred times and never really looked at. Out of nowhere, there it is.

That's the thing about ideas. You can't force them, and you can't schedule them. You can only be ready when they land.

So when it hits, catch it. Write it down. Sketch it. Hum it into a voice memo. Snap a picture. Type three words into your notes app that won't make sense to anybody but you. A notebook, a phone, a napkin, the back of a receipt. The tool doesn't matter. What matters is that you don't trust yourself to remember it later, because you won't. Everybody thinks they'll remember. Almost nobody does. The idea that felt so loud in the moment fades out quicker than you'd believe, and by the next morning all you've got left is the feeling that you had something good.

Keep it somewhere.

Not every idea is ready today

Here's where a lot of people get stuck. They get an idea, they get excited, and they feel like they have to do something with it right now. Then life happens. Work, bills, sleep, everything else that needs attention. The idea doesn't get used, and it starts to feel like a failure. Like they let it slip.

But an idea you didn't use today isn't a failure. It's a seed.

Not every idea is ready when it shows up. Some of them need time to sit. Some of them need another idea to come along and connect with them before they make sense. Some of them are waiting on skills you haven't built yet. You might write down a concept for a piece you don't have the hands to pull off this year. Keep it anyway. Next year you might.

The goal isn't to use every idea immediately. The goal is to never lose one.

Build your stash

Every element of hip hop has its own version of this.

The writer keeps a blackbook. Pages full of letters, outlines, characters, half-finished pieces. Most of it never hits a wall. But when it's time to paint, they're not starting from nothing.

The DJ builds a crate. Records pulled from everywhere, some of them sitting untouched for months or years. Then one night the right moment comes and that one record from the bottom of the stack is exactly what the room needs.

The MC keeps a rhyme book. Bars written on the bus, punchlines saved for the right verse, hooks that didn't fit the last song but might fit the next one.

The b-boy or b-girl builds up moves. Little pieces picked up in practice, in cyphers, from watching someone else. They don't all come out in one round. They come out when the music and the moment call for them.

None of these are about using everything right away. They're about collecting. Building a stash so that when the time opens up, you've got something to reach for.

That's the same energy behind Crook Lives. A lot of what ends up on a shirt or a hoodie started as a scribble or a thought that sat for a while before it was ready. Some ideas waited a long time. That's how it's supposed to work.

Use it when the time is there

Life isn't going to hand you endless free hours to create. Most people are building in the gaps. After work, late at night, on a day off, in the twenty minutes before something else starts.

That's exactly why the stash matters. When you finally get that window, you don't want to spend it staring at a blank page wondering what to make. You want to open the notebook, flip through, and find something that grabs you. The work of catching the idea is already done. Now you just get to build on it.

And sometimes you'll go back through old notes and something you wrote down months ago suddenly clicks. It didn't make sense then. It makes perfect sense now. That's one of the best feelings there is, and you only get it if you kept the idea in the first place.

Don't get frustrated

This part matters as much as anything else.

When you finally sit down with an idea, it might not come out the way you pictured it. The sketch looks off. The verse doesn't flow. The blend doesn't land. The design looked better in your head.

That's normal. That's part of it.

Frustration will tell you to quit, or to toss the idea, or that you're not good enough to pull it off. Don't listen. Put it down, step away, come back later. Try it a different way. Some ideas take three or four tries before they show you what they really want to be. Some need to go back in the stash for a while longer. Either way, it's not wasted.

Enjoy the process

At the end of the day, the finished piece is only one moment. The process is everything else. The catching, the keeping, the waiting, the building, the messing up and trying again. That's where most of your time as a creative person is actually spent.

If you only enjoy the finish line, you're going to be unhappy most of the time. But if you learn to enjoy the whole thing, every part of it becomes worth it. The random spark in the middle of the day. The notebook getting fuller. The late night session where it finally starts coming together.

So keep your eyes open. Catch what comes to you. Keep it somewhere safe. Use it when the time is right. And don't forget to enjoy the ride.
