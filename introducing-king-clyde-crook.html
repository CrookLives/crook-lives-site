King Clyde Crook

September 12, 2026 · By Karz

Every brand needs somebody watching over it who isn't in it for the crown. Crook Lives has King Clyde Crook.

Nobody crowned Clyde. He put it on himself, mostly as a joke on the idea that anybody needs one — if he's a king, everybody in the room is a king, that's the whole bit. He picked the biggest, gaudiest one he could find because a real king wouldn't have to.

He showed up the same way the culture did: uninvited, self-made, from pieces of all the elements — spray fumes drifting off a fresh wall, leftover bass rattling through the floor of somebody's basement show, the extra color left over after four elements got mixed together in one place. He didn't arrive to rule anything. There was no throne waiting, no kingdom to inherit. He just stuck around because the scene was good, the people were building something real, and nobody told him to leave.

He's shaped like he is — no sharp edges — because he was never meant to be one thing. Some nights he's the low end of a DJ's set, felt more than heard. Some nights he's the pause right before an MC lands the line everybody saw coming a mile away and still didn't expect. Some nights he's just the extra can of paint that made somebody's piece come out better than they planned. He doesn't belong to graffiti or DJing or MCing or breaking — he's what's left over when all four are happening at once and the room stops being four different things and starts being one thing.

The droopy eyes and the tongue hanging out aren't an act. That's just Clyde at rest — comfortable, unbothered, in on a joke he's not in a rush to explain. He's not the funniest one trying to prove it. He's just funny, the way someone is when they've stopped performing and started being fine with themselves. If you don't catch the joke right away, that's fine. He'll still be there when you do.

He doesn't grade anybody. He remembers not knowing how to hold a can or a mic either — remembers when his own lines were crooked, his own timing was off, his own crown would've looked ridiculous on him. When somebody's rookie tag comes out wrong or somebody's first bars fall flat, he's not the guy laughing at them. He's the guy who was there before it got good, and he already knows it's going to.

He doesn't have a crew of characters around him. He has family — the artists, DJs, writers, dancers, and everybody else who shows up and builds something real. That's the whole kingdom, and it was never his to begin with. He just happens to be wearing the hat.

--KARZ
